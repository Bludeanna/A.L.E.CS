    📚 Momentum & Collisions

    Master the key formulas and ideas before heading to the Collision Lab!

    ⚡ What is Momentum?

    Momentum (p) is the "quantity of motion" an object has. A heavy truck moving slowly can have the same momentum as a small bullet moving very fast. The harder an object is to stop, the more momentum it has!

    p = m × v

    p = momentum (kg·m/s)
    m = mass (kg)
    v = velocity (m/s)

    F = Δp / t

    F = force (N)
    Δp = change in momentum
    t = stopping time (s)

    m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'

    Conservation of Momentum — Total momentum before a collision equals total momentum after. This holds for both elastic and inelastic collisions.

    🎯 Elastic vs Inelastic Collisions
    ⚡ Elastic

    Objects bounce apart after collision. Both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved. Example: billiard balls hitting each other.

    💥 Inelastic

    Objects stick together after collision. Momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is NOT fully conserved. Example: two clay balls colliding.

    Inelastic final velocity: v' = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂) / (m₁ + m₂)

    When objects stick together their combined mass moves at this final velocity.

    🛡️ Impulse & Safety Features
    Impulse = F × t = Δp

    Impulse equals the change in momentum. Increasing stopping time reduces the force experienced — this is why airbags and crumple zones save lives!

    Safety Devices

    Seatbelts, airbags, crumple zones all work by increasing the collision time (t), which reduces force F = Δp/t and protects passengers.

    🧠 Concept Reinforcement

    Momentum is always conserved in any collision — elastic or inelastic.
    In an elastic collision, kinetic energy is also conserved; objects bounce apart.
    In an inelastic collision, objects stick together; kinetic energy is lost as heat/sound.
    F = Δp/t — increasing stopping time (with airbags, crumple zones, seatbelts) directly reduces force.
